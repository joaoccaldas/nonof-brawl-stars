## Main takeaways
### 1) Service performance is improving overall, but Norway is a major exception/problem area
- Response time is generally going in the right direction in several markets.
- Norway stands out as a deep operational problem, not just a normal underperformance.
- The conversation makes clear that Norway is not a simple capacity issue; it is also a behavior/people/leadership/process issue.

### 2) The main Norway issue is described very bluntly: people/attitude/compliance
- Service leadership says part of the challenge is a clique of older technicians with poor compliance and resistant habits.
- They link operational inefficiency to people behavior, sick leave patterns, and refusal to follow new ways of working.
- Leadership is unusually explicit that some people need to leave.
- This is one of the harshest people/performance transcripts in the batch.

### 3) Norway also exposed governance/control failures in dispatching
- They describe serious problems with the dispatcher role in Norway.
- The consequences included distorted scheduling, inefficient routing, and highly questionable behavior.
- Important meta-point: they are not only reacting to a bad person/case, they are now trying to redesign controls so the same thing cannot happen again.

### 4) The service discussion is full of operating-model tension: premium vs low cost
- They are debating whether to preserve very fast spare-part logistics / field response or reduce cost.
- This is not a theoretical tradeoff; it links directly to:
  - premium service promise
  - response-time KPI
  - freight costs
  - technician efficiency
- This is a strong example of the recurring Miele tension between premium positioning and process/cost reality.

### 5) MSP model is seen as structurally weak in some cases
- Current Miele Service Partner setup is criticized for lack of control:
  - weak customer-journey visibility
  - less control over execution quality
  - less margin capture/control
- The team is piloting a subcontractor model in Norway where Miele owns more of the customer journey and invoicing flow.
- This pilot is strategically important and may influence later wider decisions.

### 6) Freight-cost work is another example of the same structural logic
- Team is exploring cheaper/slower delivery models for technician spare parts.
- The hard question is: what service-level tradeoff is acceptable?
- Again, not just cost cutting — it is a system design question involving response times, first-time fix, and premium positioning.

### 7) RCC incident tracking is being used to distinguish signal from noise
- They explicitly note that total issue volume is a small percentage of total contacts.
- This helps counter the blanket feeling that “RCC is broken.”
- At the same time, the specific incidents still create frustration and need structured escalation/feedback loops.
- This is another example of João’s preferred pattern: quantify, isolate, then improve instead of reacting only emotionally.

### 8) First-time fix and technician behavior are major efficiency levers
- They discuss return rates, van stock, repeat visits, and technician habits.
- Strong idea: there are real savings and customer-premium gains in improving first-time fix.
- They also note large variation in technician behavior/performance.

### 9) There is a push toward one-company consistency, but local/legal constraints are real
- Travel-time policy, sick-leave car policy, and technician terms differ by country and business line.
- Leadership wants more alignment and cleaner policy logic.
- But local law, unions, and cross-border realities make it complicated.
- This is another classic broken-interface theme: what is rational system design centrally is not trivial locally.
